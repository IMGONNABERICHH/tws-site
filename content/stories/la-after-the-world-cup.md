Eight matches. More than 560,000 people through the gates in Inglewood. A Fan Festival at the Coliseum, ten official fan zones, three hundred–odd community events scattered across the region. Los Angeles hosted a World Cup and, by the numbers the tourism office is circulating, hosted it well.

Now comes the part nobody photographs: what the city actually keeps.

## The runway is unusually short

Most host cities get years to metabolize a World Cup. LA gets seven months. Super Bowl LXI lands at SoFi in February 2027. Then the 2028 Olympic and Paralympic Games — July 14–30 and August 15–27 — with opening and closing ceremonies split between SoFi and the Memorial Coliseum, the same bowl that lit the torch in 1932 and 1984.

SoFi will have hosted a World Cup, a Super Bowl, and an Olympics in three consecutive years. For the Games it gets converted into a 38,000-seat swimming venue, the largest in Olympic history. The building that just held a World Cup quarter-final becomes a pool.

There's a version of this that reads as civic bragging. There's a more useful version: the 2026 tournament functioned as a live rehearsal for Olympic-scale logistics — transit load, security perimeters, crowd flow across a region with no center. What broke in June and July is what LA28 now has eighteen months to fix.

## What's actually left behind

The honest answer is: not much infrastructure, by design. LA28 is running a venue plan built almost entirely on existing and temporary facilities. There is no new stadium. There is no white elephant.

That's the smart bet, and it also means the legacy question can't be answered by pointing at a building. What LA is left with is softer and harder to measure — a generation of kids in a city with two MLS sides, an NWSL club, and a summer where the world showed up in their neighborhood.

Whether that converts depends on things far less glamorous than a tournament: field access, coaching pipelines, whether the youth clinics that ran during the World Cup exist in November.

## The next frame

For anyone making images in this city, the interesting work over the next two years isn't inside the stadiums. It's in the gap — the Coliseum between events, the Inglewood blocks around a building that changes function every twelve months, the pickup games on public fields that will still be there in 2029 when the circus has moved to Brisbane.

The tournament is the easy picture. The city on either side of it is the harder one, and the only one that lasts.
